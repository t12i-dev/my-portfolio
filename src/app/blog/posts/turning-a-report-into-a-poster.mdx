## Start with questions, not charts

When a report is full of numbers, it's tempting to start drawing charts right away. For the [State of Global Air poster](/work/state-of-global-air-report) I started by writing down the questions a viewer might ask:

- Which risk factors cause the most deaths?
- How do men and women compare over the years?
- How does Vietnam compare with its neighbours?
- Which environmental risks matter most?

Each question became one section of the poster, and each section got exactly one chart.

## Pick the form that fits the question

Once the questions were clear, the chart types followed:

- **Comparisons across many categories** became radial charts. Wrapping the bars in a circle makes a long list of risk factors feel like a single object.
- **Change over a few years** became pairs of organic glyphs. They read faster than a table and add warmth to a heavy subject.
- **Regional comparison** became stacked columns, the clearest way to compare countries side by side.
- **Flows between categories and years** became a flow chart.

## Design on black

A dark background makes every colour more intense. I kept the palette small: gold for titles and highlights, and soft pinks, lilacs and blues for categories. With so much going on, colour has to do the sorting so the reader doesn't have to.

## Design for three distances

A poster is read from far away, from arm's length and up close. I tried to give each distance something:

1. **Far:** the gold title and the big shapes.
2. **Arm's length:** section headings like *Deaths, year, male/female*.
3. **Up close:** legends, axis values and short captions.

If a viewer only ever sees the first layer, the poster still has to work. Everything after that is a reward for looking longer.

## What I'd take to the next project

Data visualisation doesn't have to choose between being accurate and being beautiful. If every shape is driven by real numbers, you're free to be expressive with everything else.
